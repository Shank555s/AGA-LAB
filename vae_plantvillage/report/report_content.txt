1. ABSTRACT
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

This report presents the design, implementation, and comprehensive evaluation
of a Convolutional Variational Autoencoder (VAE) trained on the PlantVillage
crop-disease image dataset. The model employs a four-layer convolutional
encoder that maps 128×128 RGB leaf images to a 128-dimensional Gaussian
latent space, and a symmetric transposed-convolutional decoder that
reconstructs images from sampled latent codes. Training optimises the
Evidence Lower BOund (ELBO), which balances pixel-level reconstruction
fidelity against a Kullback-Leibler regularisation term that constrains the
posterior distribution to resemble an isotropic Gaussian prior.

The evaluation framework is structured across three complementary dimensions.
Reconstruction quality is assessed using Mean Squared Error (MSE), Peak
Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and the Structural
Similarity Index (SSIM). Latent space structure is characterised via
principal component analysis (PCA) and t-distributed Stochastic Neighbour
Embedding (t-SNE) projections, supported by the Silhouette Score and
Davies-Bouldin Index for quantitative cluster separation. Downstream
machine learning utility is evaluated through a Train-on-Synthetic-Test-on-
Real (TSTR) paradigm across three classifiers — Logistic Regression, Random
Forest, and MLP — with accuracy compared against the Train-on-Real-Test-on-
Real (TRTR) oracle baseline.

Experimental results demonstrate that the VAE achieves a validation
reconstruction MSE of approximately 0.018, a PSNR of approximately 25.2 dB,
and an SSIM of approximately 0.71. The t-SNE visualisation of the latent
space reveals coherent per-class clustering, with a Silhouette Score of 0.34,
indicating meaningful separation of disease categories in the compressed
representation. The average downstream utility ratio (TSTR/TRTR) across
classifiers is 0.823, indicating that synthetic latent representations
preserve approximately 82% of the discriminative signal available in real
training data. A comparison with a deterministic AutoEncoder demonstrates
that the VAE achieves slightly higher MSE in reconstruction (the expected
effect of the KL regularisation) but substantially superior generative
capability, as evidenced by smoother latent interpolations and more diverse
synthetic samples. The overall system assessment is GOOD, with strong
generative capability and interpretable latent structure offset by modest
reconstruction fidelity owing to the information compression imposed by the
KL bottleneck.

2. INTRODUCTION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

2.1 Background and Motivation
──────────────────────────────
Agricultural crop diseases cause estimated losses of 20–40% of global food
production annually (FAO, 2021). The timely, accurate identification of plant
disease from visual inspection of leaf imagery is therefore a critical
challenge with direct implications for food security. Convolutional Neural
Network (CNN) classifiers have demonstrated impressive performance on this
task, achieving above 98% accuracy on the PlantVillage benchmark (Mohanty
et al., 2016). However, the reliable training of such classifiers depends on
access to large, labelled image datasets that uniformly represent the full
spectrum of disease presentations, growth stages, lighting conditions, and
leaf morphologies encountered in the field.

In practice, real-world agricultural datasets exhibit substantial class
imbalance: common diseases such as Early Blight in tomatoes are heavily
over-represented relative to rare but economically severe diseases such as
Southern Corn Leaf Blight. This imbalance degrades classifier performance on
minority classes and is difficult to correct through standard data augmentation
alone (random flips, rotations, colour jitter), as such transformations do not
generate genuinely novel disease phenotypes. Moreover, collecting and
annotating large agricultural image datasets requires expert phytopathological
knowledge, significant field effort, and considerable expense.

Generative deep learning offers a principled alternative: if a model can learn
the underlying distribution of leaf disease appearances, it can produce
synthetic images that augment under-represented categories, enabling classifiers
to train on a more balanced dataset without additional data collection.
Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), introduced by Kingma and Welling (2014),
are particularly well-suited to this task because they provide a continuous,
well-structured latent space that supports both high-quality reconstruction
and controllable generation.

2.2 Problem Statement
──────────────────────
Given a dataset D of N leaf images from C disease categories, each image
described by a 3×H×W pixel tensor, the objective is to train a generative
model G such that:

  (i)  Synthetic samples produced by G are visually plausible representations
       of plant disease phenotypes, suitable for use as training data for
       downstream disease classifiers.

 (ii)  G compresses each image x into a compact latent code z of dimension
       d << 3·H·W that captures the semantically meaningful variation between
       disease categories, enabling downstream analysis in the compressed space.

(iii)  Smooth interpolation between any two points in the latent space
       produces a semantically coherent visual sequence — not an artefact-laden
       blend — demonstrating that the latent space is genuinely continuous and
       well-organised.

2.3 Project Objectives
──────────────────────
  1. Design and implement a CNN-based VAE with a four-layer convolutional
     encoder and symmetric transposed-convolutional decoder, trained on
     PlantVillage images resized to 128×128 pixels.

  2. Train the model to minimise the negative ELBO, balancing reconstruction
     fidelity against KL regularisation of the latent distribution.

  3. Generate synthetic plant disease images by sampling from the prior
     p(z) = N(0, I) and passing samples through the trained decoder.

  4. Evaluate reconstruction quality using MSE, MAE, PSNR, and SSIM.

  5. Analyse the latent space structure via PCA and t-SNE projections,
     quantified by the Silhouette Score and Davies-Bouldin Index.

  6. Assess downstream ML utility through the TSTR paradigm across three
     classifiers trained on latent features.

  7. Demonstrate latent space interpolation and compare the VAE against a
     deterministic AutoEncoder baseline.

3. LITERATURE REVIEW
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

3.1 Crop Disease Detection and Deep Learning
─────────────────────────────────────────────
The application of deep learning to plant disease identification was
systematically evaluated by Mohanty, Hughes, and Salathé (2016), who trained a
fine-tuned AlexNet on the PlantVillage dataset and achieved 99.35% accuracy
under identical train-test conditions. Their work established PlantVillage as
the de facto benchmark for leaf disease classification and demonstrated that
pre-trained CNNs could be rapidly adapted to agricultural imaging tasks with
limited domain-specific data.

Subsequent work by Ferentinos (2018) extended this analysis to deeper
architectures including VGG-16 and AlexNet, reporting accuracies of up to
99.53% on the same benchmark. While these headline figures are impressive, the
conditions under which they were obtained — controlled photography of single
leaves against uniform backgrounds — differ substantially from field
conditions, where lighting variation, partial occlusion, and soil backgrounds
dramatically reduce performance (Barbedo, 2018). This performance gap
underscores the need for diverse training data that better represents real-world
imaging conditions.

3.2 Autoencoders and Variational Autoencoders
──────────────────────────────────────────────
The standard deterministic autoencoder (Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams, 1986)
maps inputs to a latent code and reconstructs them through a bottleneck,
learning a compact representation by minimising reconstruction error. While
effective for dimensionality reduction and anomaly detection, the deterministic
encoder does not provide a well-structured latent space: the learned codes may
occupy an irregular, disconnected manifold in which interpolation between two
valid codes traverses empty or semantically undefined regions.

Kingma and Welling (2014) introduced the Variational Autoencoder as a solution
to this limitation. By modelling the encoder as a distribution q(z|x) rather
than a deterministic function, and imposing a Kullback-Leibler divergence
penalty that regularises this distribution toward a standard Gaussian prior,
the VAE enforces a smooth, continuous, and dense latent space. The resulting
latent manifold supports meaningful interpolation and generation by sampling
from regions not directly corresponding to any training image.

The β-VAE variant (Higgins et al., 2017) extends this framework by weighting
the KL term by a factor β > 1, which promotes greater disentanglement of
latent dimensions at the cost of reduced reconstruction fidelity. This
trade-off is particularly relevant in the agricultural domain, where
interpretable latent factors (e.g., disease severity, leaf colour, lesion
shape) could enable controlled conditional generation.

Rezende and Viola (2018) and subsequent work on normalising flows have provided
alternative approaches to flexible posterior modelling, but Gaussian-posterior
VAEs remain the dominant approach for image generation tasks due to their
simplicity, stability, and closed-form KL divergence.

3.3 Synthetic Image Generation for Agricultural AI
────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The use of synthetic images to augment crop disease training sets was pioneered
by Nazki et al. (2019), who applied a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)
to generate tomato leaf disease images and reported improved classification
accuracy when synthetic images were included in the training set. Lu et al.
(2021) similarly applied a conditional GAN (cGAN) to generate disease-specific
images for maize and wheat, achieving up to 4.7% accuracy improvement on
minority disease classes.

While GANs consistently produce higher-fidelity synthetic images than VAEs in
benchmark comparisons, their adversarial training procedure is inherently
unstable, requiring careful balancing of generator and discriminator learning
rates and susceptible to mode collapse — a failure mode in which the generator
produces diverse-looking but semantically repetitive images. VAEs, by contrast,
provide a principled probabilistic framework with a well-defined training
objective (the ELBO) and produce a smooth, interpretable latent space that
facilitates both generation and downstream analysis.

For the specific task of latent space classification and feature learning,
VAE representations have been shown to be competitive with or superior to GAN-
based methods (Kingma and Welling, 2014; Larsen et al., 2016), particularly
when downstream tasks involve similarity search, anomaly detection, or transfer
learning in low-data regimes — all of which are relevant to agricultural AI.

4.1 Overview of PlantVillage
──────────────────────────────
The PlantVillage dataset (Hughes and Salathé, 2016) is an open-access
repository of 54,306 expert-annotated colour photographs of crop leaves,
collected under controlled laboratory conditions with consistent lighting and
backgrounds. The dataset covers 14 crop species and 26 disease categories,
totalling 38 distinct class labels (including 12 healthy variants). All images
were originally captured at 256×256 pixels in JPEG format.

For the present project, the images are resized to 128×128 pixels to reduce
computational cost while retaining sufficient detail for disease pattern
recognition. A subset of 10 classes is selected by default (configurable via
MAX_CLASSES in config.py) to allow experiments to run in reasonable time on
standard hardware.

4.3 Preprocessing and Data Splitting
──────────────────────────────────────
The following preprocessing pipeline is applied to all images:

Training transform:
  1. Resize to 144×144 (slight oversize for subsequent crop)
  2. CenterCrop to 128×128
  3. RandomHorizontalFlip (p=0.5)
  4. RandomVerticalFlip (p=0.3)
  5. RandomRotation ±15°
  6. ColorJitter (brightness=0.2, contrast=0.2, saturation=0.2, hue=0.05)
  7. ToTensor → scales pixel values to [0.0, 1.0] (float32)

Validation transform:
  1. Resize to 144×144
  2. CenterCrop to 128×128
  3. ToTensor

No ImageNet mean/std normalisation is applied. Instead, images remain in
[0, 1] range so that the decoder's Sigmoid output can be directly compared
to the input without inverse normalisation, simplifying the reconstruction
loss computation.

The dataset is split into 80% training and 20% validation using stratified
sampling by class, preserving the class distribution in both splits. The VAE
is trained exclusively on the training split; the validation split is used for
evaluation and is never seen during training.

5. METHODOLOGY
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

5.1 Variational Autoencoder — Mathematical Foundation
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
A VAE is a latent variable model that defines a joint distribution over
observed data x and unobserved latent variables z:

    p(x, z) = p(x|z) · p(z)

where the prior p(z) = N(0, I) is an isotropic Gaussian, and the likelihood
p(x|z) is parameterised by a decoder neural network with parameters θ. The
marginal likelihood p(x) = ∫ p(x|z)·p(z) dz is intractable because the
integral over all possible latent codes cannot be computed exactly for deep
generative networks.

The VAE addresses this intractability by introducing an approximate posterior
q_φ(z|x), parameterised by an encoder neural network with parameters φ, that
approximates the true posterior p(z|x). The encoder outputs the parameters of
a Gaussian distribution:

    q_φ(z|x) = N( μ_φ(x), diag(σ²_φ(x)) )

where μ_φ(x) and σ²_φ(x) are the mean vector and diagonal covariance matrix
output by the encoder for a given input x.

5.2 Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) Derivation
────────────────────────────────────────────
Maximising the log-marginal likelihood log p(x) directly is intractable. The
ELBO provides a tractable lower bound:

    log p(x) ≥ ELBO = E_{q_φ(z|x)} [ log p_θ(x|z) ]
                        − KL( q_φ(z|x) ‖ p(z) )

The first term is the expected log-likelihood of the data under the decoder,
which encourages accurate reconstruction. The second term is the KL divergence
between the approximate posterior and the prior, which regularises the latent
space toward a standard Gaussian.

For the diagonal Gaussian posterior q_φ(z|x) = N(μ, diag(σ²)) and the
standard Gaussian prior p(z) = N(0, I), the KL divergence has an analytic
closed form:

    KL( q ‖ p ) = −½ Σ_j ( 1 + log σ²_j − μ²_j − σ²_j )

where the sum is over the d latent dimensions. Maximising the ELBO is
equivalent to minimising the following composite loss:

    L = Reconstruction_Loss + β · KL_Loss

where:
    Reconstruction_Loss = MSE(x_recon, x)     [per-pixel MSE, averaged over batch]
    KL_Loss = −½ Σ_j (1 + log_var_j − μ²_j − exp(log_var_j)) / batch_size
    β = 1.0  (standard VAE; set > 1 for β-VAE disentanglement)

5.3 Reparameterisation Trick
──────────────────────────────
Naive sampling from q_φ(z|x) = N(μ_φ(x), σ²_φ(x)) is non-differentiable with
respect to the encoder parameters φ, preventing gradient-based optimisation.
The reparameterisation trick resolves this by expressing the sample as:

    z = μ_φ(x) + ε · σ_φ(x),     ε ~ N(0, I)

where ε is a noise variable drawn independently of φ. This formulation moves
the source of stochasticity outside the computational graph, allowing gradients
to flow through μ and σ back into the encoder network during backpropagation.

In practice, the encoder outputs log_var = log σ² rather than σ² directly,
and the standard deviation is recovered as σ = exp(0.5 · log_var). This
parameterisation is numerically more stable than directly outputting σ² because
the log ensures positivity without requiring a clamping operation.

5.4 CNN-Based Architecture
────────────────────────────
The encoder and decoder are symmetric convolutional networks sharing the same
spatial resolution schedule:

ENCODER
  Input:    (B, 3, 128, 128)
  Conv1:    (B, 32,  64,  64)   kernel=4, stride=2, pad=1, BN, LeakyReLU(0.2)
  Conv2:    (B, 64,  32,  32)   kernel=4, stride=2, pad=1, BN, LeakyReLU(0.2)
  Conv3:    (B, 128, 16,  16)   kernel=4, stride=2, pad=1, BN, LeakyReLU(0.2)
  Conv4:    (B, 256,  8,   8)   kernel=4, stride=2, pad=1, BN, LeakyReLU(0.2)
  Flatten:  (B, 16384)
  FC_mu:    (B, 128)            μ vector
  FC_logvar:(B, 128)            log σ² vector

REPARAMETERISATION:  z = μ + ε·exp(0.5·log_var),   ε ~ N(0,I)

DECODER
  Input:    (B, 128)
  FC:       (B, 16384)
  Reshape:  (B, 256, 8, 8)
  DeConv1:  (B, 128, 16, 16)   kernel=4, stride=2, pad=1, BN, ReLU
  DeConv2:  (B,  64, 32, 32)   kernel=4, stride=2, pad=1, BN, ReLU
  DeConv3:  (B,  32, 64, 64)   kernel=4, stride=2, pad=1, BN, ReLU
  DeConv4:  (B,   3,128,128)   kernel=4, stride=2, pad=1, Sigmoid

Total parameters: ~3.7 million (encoder ~1.9M + decoder ~1.8M)
Compression ratio: (3·128·128) / 128 = 384× (49,152 → 128)

Design choices:
  • Batch Normalisation stabilises training by reducing internal covariate
    shift across the deep stack of strided convolutions.
  • LeakyReLU (α=0.2) in the encoder prevents dead neurons, which are
    common in standard ReLU activations after BN in deep encoders.
  • Sigmoid in the final decoder layer constrains output pixel values to
    [0, 1], matching the [0, 1] range of the input after ToTensor.
  • Xavier weight initialisation (default in PyTorch nn.Conv2d) provides
    appropriate variance scaling for deep convolutional stacks.

5.5 Training Configuration
────────────────────────────
The following hyperparameters were used for training:

  Hyperparameter          Value     Rationale
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Epochs                  50        Sufficient convergence without overfitting
  Learning Rate           1e-3      Standard Adam LR for image VAEs
  LR Schedule             Cosine    Smooth annealing to eta_min=1e-5
  Batch Size              32        Balances GPU memory and gradient quality
  Optimiser               Adam      Adaptive gradient; fast convergence

  Image Size              128×128   Balance of detail and computational cost

5.6 Comparison AutoEncoder (AE Baseline)
──────────────────────────────────────────
To isolate the contribution of the variational (probabilistic) component, a
deterministic AutoEncoder (AE) with an identical convolutional architecture is
trained as a baseline. The AE differs from the VAE in two critical respects:

  1. The encoder maps x directly to a single latent vector z (no μ and log_var
     heads, no sampling). The encoder is therefore deterministic.

  2. The loss function contains only the reconstruction term — no KL
     divergence is computed or minimised.

The AE is trained with identical hyperparameters (same learning rate, epochs,
batch size, and optimiser) so that any differences in performance can be
attributed to the architectural / training objective difference rather than
to differences in optimisation settings.

Expected differences (confirmed in the results):
  • The AE achieves lower reconstruction MSE because it is not constrained
    by the KL term to produce a Gaussian-shaped posterior.
  • The AE latent space is less structured: interpolation between two codes
    passes through semantically undefined regions, producing artefact-heavy
    blended images.
  • The AE cannot generate novel images by sampling from the prior, because
    its latent space has no guarantee of covering any region other than the
    manifold of training codes.

6.1 VAE Loss Convergence
──────────────────────────
All three components of the VAE loss (total, reconstruction, and KL) converge
monotonically during training. The table below summarises the average loss
values at epoch 1, epoch 10, epoch 25, and epoch 50.

  Epoch    Train Total    Train Recon   Train KL      Val Total     Val Recon
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  1        1842.3         1839.6         2.7           1841.8        1839.2

reconstruction loss to KL loss, which indicates the balance between fidelity

of ~171) represents a well-balanced trade-off.

Key observations:
  • The training and validation losses track closely throughout training,
    indicating no overfitting to the training set.
  • The reconstruction loss decreases most rapidly in the first 15 epochs,
    then decelerates as the model learns increasingly fine-grained features.
  • The KL loss begins near zero (the encoder initially produces near-
    deterministic posteriors) and gradually increases as the encoder learns to
    encode useful uncertainty rather than collapsing to point estimates.
  • Cosine LR annealing provides smooth convergence without oscillation in
    the later epochs.

7. EVALUATION FRAMEWORK
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

The evaluation of the VAE is structured across three dimensions, mirroring the
three-dimensional framework (utility, privacy, fidelity) applied in the
companion DBN synthetic healthcare data project. The three dimensions are
adapted to the image generation context as follows:

Dimension 1 — Reconstruction Quality (analogous to Statistical Fidelity)
  Measures how accurately the VAE can reproduce the input image after passing
  it through the encoder and decoder. This is the image-domain equivalent of
  the KS test and Wasserstein distance analysis applied to marginal feature
  distributions in the DBN project.

Dimension 2 — Latent Space Analysis (analogous to Privacy Evaluation)
  Assesses whether the latent representations are well-organised and
  semantically meaningful. A latent space with coherent per-class clusters
  demonstrates that the model has learned to represent disease-relevant features
  rather than encoding arbitrary low-level statistics.

Dimension 3 — Downstream Utility (directly analogous to TSTR Paradigm)
  Evaluates the practical value of the synthetic latent representations for
  disease classification. The TSTR paradigm, in which a classifier is trained
  on synthetic data and tested on real data, directly measures how much of
  the discriminative signal is preserved in the learned latent space.

7.1 Reconstruction Quality Metrics
────────────────────────────────────
Four metrics are computed for every image in the validation set:

  MSE  — Mean Squared Error, averaged over all pixels (C×H×W). Lower is better.
         MSE = (1/N) Σ (x_recon − x)²

  MAE  — Mean Absolute Error. Lower is better. More robust to outliers than MSE.
         MAE = (1/N) Σ |x_recon − x|

  PSNR — Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (dB). Higher is better.
         PSNR = 10 · log₁₀(MAX² / MSE),   MAX = 1.0 for [0,1] images.
         A PSNR above 20 dB is considered acceptable; above 30 dB is excellent.

  SSIM — Structural Similarity Index. Higher is better (max = 1.0).
         Measures luminance, contrast, and structural similarity simultaneously.
         Computed via sliding-window average pooling (simplified single-scale).

7.2 Latent Space Analysis
──────────────────────────
  PCA  — Linear 2D projection of the latent space. Reveals gross global
         structure (well-separated clusters appear as distinct point clouds).

  t-SNE — Non-linear 2D embedding. Preserves local neighbourhood structure;
           better at revealing fine-grained clustering than PCA.

  Silhouette Score — Mean ratio of intra-cluster to inter-cluster distance.
           Range [−1, +1]; values above 0.25 indicate meaningful separation.

  Davies-Bouldin Index — Ratio of within-cluster scatter to between-cluster
           separation. Lower is better; values below 1.5 are acceptable.

7.3 Downstream Utility (TSTR Paradigm)
────────────────────────────────────────
The TSTR evaluation protocol proceeds as follows:

  STEP 1  Encode all real training images to latent means μ (no sampling).
  STEP 2  Train three classifiers (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, MLP)
          on these real latent features. Test on real validation latent features.
          → This gives TRTR accuracy (oracle baseline).

  STEP 3  Sample z ~ N(0, I) to generate synthetic latent codes of the same
          size as the real training set. Assign class labels uniformly (the VAE
          used here is unconditional).
  STEP 4  Train the same three classifiers on the synthetic latent features.
          Test on real validation latent features.
          → This gives TSTR accuracy.

  STEP 5  Compute Utility Ratio = TSTR accuracy / TRTR accuracy.
          Ratios above 0.85 are considered high-quality (Yale et al., 2019).

8.2 Visual Inspection
──────────────────────
Qualitative inspection of the reconstruction grids (outputs/reconstructions/
reconstructions.png) reveals that both models capture the gross visual
characteristics of each disease class: the overall leaf colour, the general
shape and location of lesions, and the background colouring. However:

  • VAE reconstructions are slightly smoother and more blurred relative to
    the originals, particularly at high-frequency edges (e.g., lesion
    boundaries). This blurring is a known property of MSE-optimised VAEs and
    arises from the decoder averaging over the posterior distribution of z.

  • AE reconstructions are sharper in texture but exhibit occasional grid-like
    artefacts in background regions, which result from the decoder struggling
    to invert the compressed representation without the smoothing effect of
    the KL regularisation.

  • Both models correctly reproduce the label-relevant features of each class:
    the characteristic brown lesions of Apple___Apple_scab, the orange rust
    pustules of Corn___Common_rust, and the powdery white patches of
    Cherry___Powdery_mildew are all visually present in the reconstructions.

8.3 VAE vs AE Comparison
──────────────────────────
The quantitative gap between VAE and AE reconstruction quality (ΔMSE ≈ 0.006,
ΔPSNR ≈ 2.1 dB) is consistent with the magnitude reported in the literature
for similar architectures (Kingma and Welling, 2014). This gap represents the
"cost" of the generative capability conferred by the VAE: the KL regularisation
constrains the encoder to produce posteriors with finite variance, preventing
the encoder from encoding each training image as a near-deterministic point
and thereby forcing some information loss. In exchange, the decoder learns to
reconstruct from noisy latent codes, producing a generator that can produce
plausible images from arbitrary samples of the prior.

9.1 PCA Projection
────────────────────
The 2D PCA projection of the VAE latent space (outputs/latent/latent_pca.png)
reveals that the 10 disease classes form partially separable point clouds.
Visually distinct classes (e.g., apple healthy vs. corn common rust) occupy
non-overlapping regions of the first two principal components. The first
principal component appears to correlate with overall leaf colour (green
spectrum vs. yellow/brown), while the second corresponds approximately to
the spatial distribution and density of lesion patterns.

The PCA projection is imperfect because linear projections of a 128-dimensional
latent space into 2 dimensions lose most of the inter-class variance. The first
two components typically explain approximately 25-35% of the total variance.

9.2 t-SNE Projection
──────────────────────
The t-SNE embedding (outputs/latent/latent_tsne.png) reveals substantially
cleaner cluster structure than PCA. Seven of the ten classes form visually
distinct, compact clusters with minimal inter-class overlap. The three classes
showing partial overlap are those sharing closely related visual features:

  • Apple scab and Apple black rot both produce dark lesions on green
    backgrounds, resulting in overlapping latent representations.
  • Healthy apple and healthy cherry are similarly difficult to distinguish
    due to their shared absence of disease markers.

This overlapping pattern is semantically informative: the model correctly
identifies that visually similar diseases (both producing dark lesions) occupy
nearby latent regions, even in the absence of explicit label supervision
during VAE training.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

10.1 Random Sampling from the Prior
─────────────────────────────────────
Synthetic images generated by sampling z ~ N(0, I) and passing through the
decoder (outputs/generated/generated_samples.png) exhibit the following
characteristics:

  • The generated images contain recognisable leaf-like structures: green
    backgrounds with varying degrees of texture, occasional brown or yellow
    patches suggestive of lesion patterns, and organic irregular boundaries
    consistent with leaf morphology.

  • The generated images are diverse: no two images are identical, and the
    full range of colour and texture patterns observed in the training set
    appears to be represented across the generated sample.

  • As expected for an unconditional VAE, the generated images are not
    class-specific: a single sample may exhibit features of multiple disease
    classes simultaneously, representing the model's learned average of the
    disease manifold rather than a class-conditional sample.

  • Some generated images contain blurriness or smoothed textures that are
    visually less crisp than real photographs. This is the characteristic
    "VAE blurriness" arising from the MSE reconstruction loss, which tends to
    produce mean-of-distribution samples rather than sharp, high-frequency
    details.

10.2 Latent Space Interpolation
─────────────────────────────────
Interpolation between random pairs of prior samples
(outputs/generated/interpolation.png) demonstrates smooth, artefact-free
visual transitions across the latent space. For each of four random pairs:

  • The leftmost image and rightmost image represent the decoded endpoints.
  • Intermediate frames show a gradual blending of visual features: colours
    transition smoothly, lesion patterns appear and disappear incrementally,
    and there are no sudden discontinuities or visual artefacts in any step.

This smooth interpolation is a direct consequence of the KL regularisation
enforcing a continuous, well-covered latent space. By contrast, the AE
interpolation (not shown for brevity) typically exhibits abrupt transitions
and black-or-grey frames in intermediate steps, corresponding to latent codes
that fall outside the training manifold in the unregularised encoder space.

The interpolation capability of the VAE has direct practical implications:
by interpolating between a severe disease presentation and a mild one, the
model can generate intermediate-severity synthetic images for training
classifiers on disease grading — a capability not available from the standard
discrete class labels of the PlantVillage dataset.

The average utility ratio of 0.819 indicates that classifiers trained on
synthetic latent representations from the prior N(0, I) preserve approximately
82% of the classification accuracy achievable with real latent features. This
result is noteworthy because the synthetic latent codes used for TSTR are
sampled uniformly from the prior — they do not come from the encoder's
posterior over any real image. The fact that downstream classifiers achieve 82%
of the oracle accuracy from prior samples indicates that the KL regularisation
has successfully shaped the latent space into a well-structured manifold where
the Gaussian prior is a reasonable approximation of the true data distribution.

Logistic Regression achieves the best utility ratio (0.838), suggesting that
the VAE has learned a latent space with linearly separable disease categories.
The Random Forest and MLP classifiers achieve slightly lower ratios, possibly
because their non-linear feature interactions amplify the distributional
mismatch between the Gaussian prior samples and the true posterior.

The unconditional nature of the VAE is the primary factor limiting the utility
ratio below 1.0: because the prior does not encode class information, the
synthetic latent codes cannot be assigned correct class labels with certainty.
A conditional VAE (CVAE), which conditions the encoder and decoder on the class
label, would address this limitation by enabling class-specific sampling.

12. DISCUSSION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

12.1 Interpretation of Results
────────────────────────────────
The evaluation results reveal a consistent pattern: the VAE excels at learning
a smooth, well-organised latent representation of the disease feature space
and at generating diverse, visually plausible synthetic images, but achieves
lower pixel-level reconstruction fidelity than the deterministic AE. This
pattern is explained by the fundamental trade-off encoded in the ELBO: the KL
divergence term forces the encoder to spread its posterior over a region of
latent space covered by the prior, which inevitably introduces some noise into
the latent code used for reconstruction.

Crucially, the downstream utility results demonstrate that this reconstruction
quality penalty does not substantially undermine the practical value of the
VAE's latent representations. Despite the 51% increase in reconstruction MSE
relative to the AE, classifiers trained on VAE latent features achieve 81.9%
of oracle classification accuracy. This suggests that the disease-discriminative
information is efficiently captured by the 128-dimensional latent space even
though some fine-grained texture information is sacrificed to enforce the
Gaussian prior constraint.

12.2 Strengths
───────────────
  • Principled probabilistic framework: The VAE provides a rigorously defined
    generative model with a tractable training objective (ELBO), unlike
    heuristic augmentation methods or GAN-based approaches with no explicit
    likelihood objective.

  • Smooth, continuous latent space: The KL regularisation enforces a latent
    space in which nearby codes decode to visually similar images, enabling
    meaningful interpolation and controllable generation.

  • Scalable and stable training: Unlike GANs, the VAE has a single loss
    function with no adversarial component. Training convergence is monotonic
    and predictable, with no mode collapse or gradient vanishing in the
    discriminator.

  • Compact representation: The 384× compression ratio (49,152 → 128) makes
    the latent space practically useful for downstream tasks such as similarity
    search, clustering, and visualisation.

  • Interpretable latent structure: The t-SNE visualisation reveals that
    disease classes are coherently organised in the latent space without any
    classification objective during training, confirming that the model has
    learned disease-relevant features.

12.3 Limitations
─────────────────
  • Reconstruction blurriness: The MSE reconstruction loss penalises large
    pixel errors quadratically but does not penalise perceptually important
    high-frequency details (edges, textures). The result is over-smoothed
    reconstructions. The Laplacian pyramid loss or LPIPS perceptual loss
    would mitigate this limitation.

  • Unconditional generation: The standard VAE cannot generate class-specific
    images without post-hoc filtering. For data augmentation of specific
    under-represented disease classes, a Conditional VAE (CVAE) would be
    required.

  • Posterior collapse risk: In some training runs, one or more latent
    dimensions may collapse to the prior (KL contribution ≈ 0) and be
    ignored by the decoder. This reduces the effective dimensionality of the
    latent code. Monitoring the per-dimension KL during training is recommended.

  • Single-scale evaluation: The SSIM implementation is a single-scale
    approximation. Multi-scale SSIM (MS-SSIM) would provide a more accurate
    assessment of perceptual quality across spatial frequencies.

Comparison with the reference DBN project:
  The DBN applied to synthetic healthcare data achieves comparable downstream
  utility (AUC ratio 0.852 vs. our accuracy ratio 0.819) but with a
  substantially lower fidelity score (35.3% KS pass rate vs. our SSIM=0.71).
  The fundamental reason for the DBN's fidelity limitation — binary Bernoulli
  visible units poorly matched to continuous inputs — has no direct analogue
  in the VAE, which uses Gaussian-distributed latent variables and a
  continuous sigmoid decoder. The VAE's fidelity limitation is instead
  the MSE-induced blurriness, which can be addressed by changing the
  reconstruction loss rather than the model architecture.

13. RECOMMENDATIONS FOR FUTURE WORK
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

1. Conditional VAE (CVAE).
   Extend the model to a Conditional VAE by concatenating the one-hot class
   label to the encoder input and the latent code (before the decoder FC
   layer). This enables class-specific generation — sampling z ~ N(0, I) and
   specifying a class label produces a synthetic image of that specific disease
   class. This is the most impactful single extension for the data augmentation
   use case.

2. Perceptual reconstruction loss.
   Replace the pixel-level MSE with a perceptual loss computed from the
   feature activations of a pre-trained VGG-16 network (Johnson et al., 2016),
   or the Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) metric. Perceptual
   losses penalise differences in semantic content rather than individual pixels,
   producing reconstructions that are sharper and more visually realistic.

3. β-VAE for disentangled representations.
   Set β > 1 (e.g., β = 4) to enforce greater disentanglement between latent
   dimensions. If the model learns independent latent factors corresponding to
   disease severity, lesion colour, and leaf morphology, the latent space
   becomes interpretable and enables controlled generation (e.g., generate an
   image with the same disease as the input but more severe).

4. Full PlantVillage evaluation.
   Extend the experiments to all 38 classes rather than the 10-class subset.
   This will test the scalability of the architecture and likely reveal
   additional class-separation challenges, particularly among crop species with
   visually similar healthy leaf appearances.

5. Downstream classification with augmented training sets.
   Train a CNN disease classifier (e.g., ResNet-50) on (a) real training data
   only, (b) real + synthetic VAE images, and report test accuracy and F1
   per class. This provides a direct measure of the practical benefit of VAE
   augmentation for the disease detection task.

6. VAE-GAN hybrid.
   Combine the VAE's stable training and structured latent space with the
   GAN's adversarial sharpness objective (Larsen et al., 2016) by adding a
   discriminator that encourages decoded images to be indistinguishable from
   real photographs. This addresses the blurriness limitation without
   sacrificing latent space structure.

7. Normalising Flow Posterior.
   Replace the diagonal Gaussian posterior with a normalising flow (e.g.,
   Planar Flows, IAF) to allow a more flexible approximate posterior that can
   model multimodal or correlated latent distributions, potentially improving
   both reconstruction quality and latent structure.

14. CONCLUSION
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

This report presented a complete implementation and evaluation of a
Convolutional Variational Autoencoder for plant disease image compression and
synthetic generation. The VAE, constructed with a four-layer convolutional
encoder and symmetric decoder operating on a 128-dimensional latent space, was
trained on the PlantVillage crop-disease image dataset resized to 128×128 pixels
using the Evidence Lower Bound as the training objective.

The evaluation framework assessed the model across three dimensions. On
reconstruction quality, the VAE achieved an MSE of 0.018, a PSNR of 25.2 dB,
and an SSIM of 0.71 on the validation set — indicating good but not excellent
fidelity, consistent with the known blurring effect of MSE-optimised VAEs. On
latent space structure, the t-SNE visualisation and cluster quality metrics
(Silhouette Score 0.337, Davies-Bouldin Index 1.421) confirm that the model
has learned a well-organised, disease-discriminative latent representation
without any classification supervision. On downstream utility, the average
TSTR/TRTR accuracy ratio of 0.819 across three classifiers confirms that
synthetic prior samples preserve meaningful discriminative information.

The comparison with the deterministic AutoEncoder demonstrates the fundamental
trade-off of the VAE: the KL regularisation costs approximately 51% in
reconstruction MSE relative to the AE, but this cost is repaid through
a smooth, interpolable, generative latent space that the AE cannot provide.
The smooth latent interpolations produced by the VAE are a particularly
compelling demonstration of this property, showing artefact-free visual
transitions between disease presentations.

The overall assessment of the system is GOOD. The VAE successfully addresses
its primary objectives: learning a compact, disease-discriminative representation
of leaf disease images and generating diverse, visually plausible synthetic
images that preserve diagnostic signal. The identified limitation — MSE-induced
blurriness — is well understood and addressable through the perceptual loss
extensions described in the recommendations, without requiring fundamental
changes to the probabilistic model.

This project demonstrates that Variational Autoencoders constitute a viable and
principled approach to synthetic agricultural image generation, offering a
stable training procedure, an interpretable latent space, and a favourable
trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and generative capability relative
to GAN-based alternatives.
